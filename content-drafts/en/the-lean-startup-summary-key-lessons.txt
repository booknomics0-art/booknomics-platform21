#HOOK
Most startups fail not because they cannot build, but because they build something nobody wants. Eric Ries offers a method for finding out early and cheaply, before the money and the years are gone.

#SUMMARY
### Six months of work that nobody wanted

In 2004, Eric Ries helped found a company called IMVU, which let people chat with each other as 3D avatars. The team spent about six months building a product they were certain would succeed. It connected to the big instant-messaging networks, and it was full of features. When the first customers arrived, there were very few of them, and they did not seem to care. Ries looked back and saw that the months had been spent on a guess, not on knowledge.

That story is the seed of The Lean Startup, published in September 2011. Ries asks a blunt question. If most of what a new company does is build things that customers never wanted, what was all that effort for? His answer is that the work counts only if it teaches you something true about customers. He calls that validated learning, and he builds an entire way of working around it.

He borrows the name and spirit from lean manufacturing, the approach Toyota developed under the engineer Taiichi Ohno, which eliminates waste. In a startup, he says, the biggest waste is not idle machines. It is making products that people do not want. The second source is the mentor Steve Blank, whose idea of customer development argued that founders must get out of the building and test their assumptions with real customers.

### What counts as a startup

Ries defines a startup as a human institution designed to create a new product or service under conditions of extreme uncertainty. The words that matter are the last ones. A corner shop opening in a familiar market is a business, but not necessarily a startup. A startup does not know who its customer is or what the product should be. Traditional management tools, such as detailed plans and forecasts, work well when you know a lot about the future. They fail when you know almost nothing.

This is why Ries says a startup needs a different kind of management. Plans, budgets and milestones do not help you when the real question is whether anybody will pay. You need an approach built to find the answer quickly, and you need to treat the early stage as a scientific experiment rather than a rollout.

### The core loop: build, measure, learn

The heart of the method is a feedback loop with three steps. You build a small version of the product, you measure how customers respond, and you learn whether your idea holds up. Then you decide what to do next. The objective is to speed through the loop as quickly as possible, because the faster you learn, the less you waste.

Ries points out that most teams think in the order build, measure, learn, but he suggests planning in reverse. Start with what you need to learn. Then decide what to measure. Only then decide what to build. This turns the product into a tool for answering a question, not a finished creation to be admired.

### The minimum viable product

The best-known idea in the book is the minimum viable product, or MVP. It is the version of a product that lets you collect the maximum amount of validated learning with the least effort. It is not a low-quality product, and it is not necessarily small. It is the smallest experiment that can test your riskiest assumption.

The examples are the book's charm. Drew Houston of Dropbox was worried that people would not understand file syncing until they saw it. Instead of building the full product, he made a short video demonstrating how it would work. The waiting list reportedly grew from 5,000 to 75,000 people overnight. Nick Swinmurn, who started Zappos in 1999, tested the idea of selling shoes online by photographing shoes in local stores and posting the pictures. When someone ordered, he went to the store, bought the pair and shipped it. He had no warehouse and no stock, but he learned that people would buy shoes online. A company called Food on the Table, led by Manuel Rosso, began by serving a handful of families personally. The team did the planning by hand, which Ries calls a concierge MVP, so that they could learn what customers valued before building software.

Ries adds that the MVP must be embarrassing to you, at least a little. He quotes Reid Hoffman, the LinkedIn founder, who said that if you are not embarrassed by the first version of your product, you launched too late. The reason is simple. Every feature you add before you have learned something is a bet you have not tested.

### Validated learning and leap-of-faith assumptions

Every startup rests on leap-of-faith assumptions, the beliefs that would have to be true for the business to work. Ries names two of them. The value hypothesis asks whether the product really delivers value to customers who use it. The growth hypothesis asks how new customers will find the product. Both can be tested.

The test must produce evidence, not opinions. Ries warns against what he calls success theater, such as using press coverage or a big launch to feel good while learning nothing. Real validation looks like customers paying, returning or recommending the product.

He describes the discipline of innovation accounting, a way of measuring progress when you have few customers and little revenue. You start by using an MVP to establish a baseline. Then you try to tune the engine, improving the numbers with experiments. Finally, you decide whether to pivot or persevere, depending on whether the numbers are moving.

### Vanity metrics and actionable ones

One of the most practical sections separates vanity metrics from actionable metrics. Total registered users, cumulative page views and gross revenue all go up and to the right, which feels good, but they do not tell you whether your decisions are working. Actionable metrics link cause and effect. For example, if you change the signup flow on Monday, you should be able to see whether Tuesday's new users behave differently from Monday's.

Ries recommends cohort analysis, which tracks groups of customers who joined at the same time. He describes an educational company, Grockit, whose founder Farbood Nivi found that adding features did not raise the conversion rate of new cohorts. That was a painful finding, because the team had been working hard and the total numbers were growing. The cohort view showed that the learning was not improving.

He also favors split testing, where two groups of customers see different versions of a product at the same time. Each test gives a clear result, and a team that runs many small tests learns much faster than one that waits for large launches.

### Pivot or persevere

At regular intervals, the team must decide whether to continue its strategy or to change course without changing the vision. A pivot, in Ries's words, is a structured course correction designed to test a new fundamental hypothesis. He lists several types. A zoom-in pivot turns one feature into the whole product. A zoom-out pivot makes the old product a feature of a bigger one. A customer segment pivot keeps the product but changes who it is for. Others change the platform, the business architecture, the value capture, the engine of growth or the technology.

The hardest part is the decision itself. Founders become attached to the original idea, and small improvements can look like proof that all is well. Ries recommends a regular pivot-or-persevere meeting, at which the team looks at the data honestly and asks whether it is moving toward a sustainable business.

### Small batches and engines of growth

From lean manufacturing, Ries takes the lesson of small batches. He uses the example of stuffing newsletters into envelopes. Doing all the folding first, then the stuffing, then the sealing seems efficient. But if you complete one envelope at a time, you find problems, such as a newsletter that does not fit, after one envelope rather than after a thousand. In software, this means releasing small changes often, which is the idea behind continuous deployment.

He also describes three engines of growth. The sticky engine grows by keeping customers for a long time. The viral engine grows because customers bring others in as a side effect of using the product. The paid engine grows by spending advertising money, provided that the lifetime value of a customer exceeds the cost of acquiring them. A startup should focus on one engine at a time and test the numbers that matter for it.

Finally, the book borrows the five whys from Toyota. When something goes wrong, ask why, and keep asking until you find the root cause, which is usually a human or process problem rather than a technical one. Then invest in a proportionate fix at each level.

### Where the book is weaker

The Lean Startup is often misunderstood, and some of the blame lies in its slogans. "MVP" has become an excuse to ship sloppy products. Ries does not intend that. But the book offers little guidance for domains where an experiment is expensive or risky, such as medicine, hardware or regulated finance. A test with real patients cannot be run the way a landing page can.

There is also a danger of local optimization. Testing tiny changes can improve an idea that is fundamentally limited, and customer feedback does not always reveal what people will want once it exists. Many of the book's stories are told by the founders themselves, which introduces survivor bias. Even so, the central discipline, test your riskiest assumption first and measure honestly, is hard to argue with.

#KEY_INSIGHTS
**1. Progress is learning, not shipping.** A team can release features every week and still learn nothing. Ries treats validated learning as the unit of progress. Ask your team on Monday what you will know by Friday that you do not know now. If nobody can answer, you are building on hope, not evidence.

**2. A startup is an experiment, not a smaller big company.** Case: IMVU spent six months on a product that customers did not value. The lesson is that detailed plans do not help when the market is unknown. Question for Monday: which three assumptions in our plan would destroy the business if wrong, and how cheaply can we test each?

**3. The MVP exists to answer a question.** Zappos did not need a warehouse to learn that people would buy shoes online. Swinmurn posted photographs and fulfilled orders by hand. Ask yourself what the smallest thing is that would prove or disprove your riskiest assumption, and build only that.

**4. Talk to customers, but watch what they do.** Steve Blank's customer development says to leave the building. Interviews reveal problems, but behavior reveals priorities. Dropbox did not ask people whether they wanted file sync. It showed a video and counted sign-ups. Question: what action, not opinion, would show that a customer cares?

**5. Vanity metrics flatter you.** A growing total of registered users feels like success while hiding whether your changes work. Grockit's cohort data showed that new users were not converting better, even though the totals rose. Question: can we tie this number to a specific change we made last week?

**6. Measure cohorts, not totals.** Compare groups who joined at the same time to learn whether the product is improving. Totals hide trends because old customers dominate. Ask your analyst for a simple cohort table of this month's signups against last month's, tracking the same action.

**7. Small batches reveal problems faster.** The envelope example shows that a team that does one complete unit first finds the error early. In software or services, release small changes frequently. Question: what is the smallest complete version of this work that we could put in front of a customer this week?

**8. Decide to pivot with data, not mood.** A pivot changes strategy while keeping the vision. Teams avoid it because it feels like failure, yet a pivot made early saves years. Hold a regular pivot-or-persevere meeting, and write the decision down with the evidence you used.

**9. Choose one engine of growth.** Sticky, viral and paid engines need different numbers. Spreading effort across all three dilutes learning. Ask which engine fits your product, and track the single metric that shows it is working, such as retention, invitations per user or cost per acquired customer.

**10. Ask why five times.** When a failure occurs, dig until you find the root cause. The first answer is usually a symptom, while the fifth is often a process gap. Toyota used this to make fixes proportionate. The next time a release fails, hold a fifteen-minute five-whys review without blaming anyone.

**11. Be honest about your limits.** The method works best where experiments are cheap. If yours are costly, find cheaper proxies, such as prototypes, interviews or simulations, before committing to the large build. Question: what is the cheapest experiment that would change our mind?

**12. Celebrate learning out loud.** Teams tend to celebrate launches, because they are visible. Ries argues that the useful milestone is the experiment that changed your mind, even when the result was bad news. Make that visible. At the end of each week, hold a five-minute session where each person names one belief that was confirmed and one that was killed. Over time, the team learns that finding out you were wrong early is a win, not an embarrassment, and it stops hiding bad numbers. A company that punishes honest negative results will soon stop receiving them, and then its dashboards will look healthy right up until the money runs out. Protect the messenger, and reward the question that saved a quarter.

#APPLY_TODAY
Write down the single riskiest assumption behind your current project, in one sentence beginning "We believe that customers will..." Then design a test you could run within a week that costs less than one day of your team's time, such as a landing page, ten customer calls or a manual version of the service. Decide now what result would make you change course. Put that number in writing so that you cannot argue yourself out of it later. Share it with one colleague or advisor and ask them to hold you to it. If the result lands below your line, your job is not to explain it away but to choose the next experiment, which might be a new price, a new customer group or a simpler offer.

7. Where in your work would a five-whys review reveal a root cause you keep avoiding?
8. Which belief about your customers have you never challenged because it feels too obvious, and how could you test it this month?

#ACTION_SYSTEM
This is a seven-week plan for a small team or a solo founder with one product idea. Each week has one question, one task and one deliverable. The imaginary company is Loaf, a subscription service for fresh bread, but you should substitute your own idea.

### Week 1: Name the assumptions
Question: what must be true for this business to work? Task: list every assumption, then mark the two riskiest, one about value and one about growth. Deliverable: a one-page list with the two leap-of-faith assumptions written as testable statements. For Loaf, one might be "Busy households will pay $30 a month for weekly bread."

### Week 2: Talk to ten customers
Question: does the problem exist, and how do people solve it today? Task: hold ten short conversations with likely customers and ask about past behavior, not opinions. Deliverable: a summary table showing what each person does now, what they spend and what frustrates them.

### Week 3: Build the smallest test
Question: what is the cheapest way to see real behavior? Task: create a landing page, a manual service or a short video that offers the product. Deliverable: a live test with one clear action, such as a pre-order or a signup. For Loaf, that might be a page and five hand-delivered loaves.

### Week 4: Run it and record the baseline
Question: what do real numbers say? Task: send fifty to two hundred real people to the test and record the result. Deliverable: a baseline metric, such as the percentage who pre-ordered, with the date and the number of people.

### Week 5: Change one thing
Question: which change might improve the baseline? Task: change one element, such as the price, the headline or the offer, and run it with a fresh group. Deliverable: a before-and-after comparison with the same definitions, so you can see if the change helped.

### Week 6: Look at cohorts and retention
Question: do customers come back? Task: follow the people who took the first step and record whether they continue. Deliverable: a simple cohort table with at least two cohorts.

### Week 7: Pivot or persevere
Question: is the evidence moving toward a sustainable business? Task: hold a one-hour meeting, review the data and choose to continue, adjust or change direction. Deliverable: a written decision with three reasons and the next experiment.

#AUDIO_SCRIPT
Let me begin with a story about wasted time. In 2004, a small team in California spent about six months building a product. They were smart, hardworking and sure of themselves. When they finally launched, almost nobody cared. One of the founders was Eric Ries, and the experience was painful enough that it led him to write The Lean Startup.

His big question is simple. Why do so many new companies fail, even when the people are talented? His answer is that they build things nobody wants. And the cure is not to build faster. It is to learn faster.

Here is the central loop. Build, measure, learn. You build a small version of your idea, you put it in front of real people, you measure what they do, and you learn whether your assumption was right. Then you decide what to do next. Ries suggests thinking about it backward. Start by asking what you need to learn. Then decide what to measure. Then build only what lets you measure it.

That leads to the minimum viable product, the MVP. People misunderstand this. It is not a bad product. It is the smallest experiment that can test your riskiest assumption. Consider Zappos. Nick Swinmurn wanted to know whether people would buy shoes online. He did not build a warehouse. He photographed shoes in local stores and posted the pictures. When someone ordered, he went to the store, bought the shoes and mailed them. Was that efficient? No. But it answered the question. Or think about Dropbox. Drew Houston made a short video showing how the product would work, and the waiting list reportedly jumped from five thousand to seventy-five thousand people overnight. A video is a very cheap experiment.

Now let me introduce the idea I find most useful, which is the difference between vanity metrics and actionable ones. A vanity metric is a number that goes up and feels good. Total users. Total page views. The problem is that totals almost always go up, even when your decisions are bad. An actionable metric is something you can connect to a change. If you change your signup page on Monday, do Tuesday's new users behave differently? That is why Ries likes cohorts, which are groups of customers who joined at the same time. One company he describes, Grockit, kept adding features, yet each new cohort converted no better than the last. The total numbers looked great. The cohorts told the truth.

Then there is the pivot. A pivot is not giving up. It is changing your strategy while keeping your vision, based on what you have learned. Maybe one feature is what customers actually love, and you make it the whole product. Maybe you keep the product and change the customer. The hard part is admitting it. So Ries suggests a regular meeting, where the team looks at the data and decides whether to pivot or persevere. Put the decision in writing.

Two more tools. First, small batches. Imagine stuffing a thousand envelopes. It feels efficient to fold everything first. But if you do one envelope from start to finish, you find the problem early, maybe that the paper does not fit. Release small changes often. Second, the five whys. When something breaks, ask why, and keep asking. The first answer is usually a symptom. By the fifth, you often reach a process problem, and you fix that rather than blaming a person.

Now, honest criticism. The word MVP has been used as an excuse to ship sloppy products, and that is not what Ries meant. The method works best when experiments are cheap. If you are building medical devices, or a bank, you cannot test with real customers the way you can test a landing page. Also, tiny tests can trap you in a small improvement on a limited idea. And most of the stories in the book are told by the founders themselves, so there is survivor bias.

So how would you use it? Imagine a small company called Loaf that delivers fresh bread. The founders think busy families will pay thirty dollars a month. Instead of building an app, they write a simple page and deliver five loaves by hand. They count how many people pay again in the second week. That is validated learning. It is cheap, it is fast, and it might save them a year.

One more thought before the homework. Ries wrote this book for people who feel pressure to look certain. Investors want confidence. Teams want direction. But the honest state of a new idea is that you do not know yet. The method gives you a respectable way to say so. You can tell your team, "We do not know if customers will pay, so this month we will find out, and here is how." That is not weakness. It is what makes a plan believable.

Here is your homework. Write down the riskiest assumption in your current project. Design a test that costs less than a day of effort. And decide, before you run it, what result would make you change course. That last part is the real discipline. A test you will ignore is just theater.
