#TAGLINE
The most famous play ever written: a prince who must avenge his father — and who thinks instead.

#SUMMARY
### The Ghost's Demand

Hamlet begins the way dozens of revenge plays of its era began — with a ghost on the battlements of Elsinore demanding blood — and then, in a move that made modern drama possible, it asks whether the ghost should be believed. The play's plot fits in a paragraph: the King of Denmark has died; his brother Claudius has married the widow, Queen Gertrude, and taken the throne; the dead king's son, Prince Hamlet, has returned from university in Wittenberg to a court that has already moved on. A spirit resembling his father walks the night walls, and when Hamlet confronts it, the ghost tells him the unbearable: "The serpent that did sting thy father's life / Now wears his crown." Murder, fratricide, incest — the ghost demands revenge, and fast, "before the night joint-laborer with the day / In travel" burns away.

Everything the play is famous for grows from one structural decision: Shakespeare gives his hero a reason to hesitate. In Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy, the genre's model, the revenger gets on with it. Here, the ghost may be honest — or it may be "a devil" who "abuses me to damn me," exploiting a son's filial duty to trick him into murdering a king and dying damned. Hamlet cannot verify the claim by any means available to him. The play that follows is what happens when the plot of a revenge tragedy is handed to a man with a philosopher's standards of evidence, and the result is the first great psychological drama in Western literature: a genre about action rebuilt as a genre about the interior of the man who cannot act.

Shakespeare also stacks the deck against the audience's easy verdicts. Elsinore is genuinely rotten — Claudius is a murderer who prays and cannot mean his prayers ("My words fly up, my thoughts remain below"), who has corrupted courtiers, bought the vote of the council, and wived his brother's queen inside a month. But the court is also, on its surface, functional: Claudius is an effective politician, a better diplomat than the old king, and publicly charming. The play's unbearable intelligence is that the correct man for the throne and the murderer are the same man, and only Hamlet and the audience know it — a burden the play calls "the time is out of joint. O cursed spite, / That ever I was born to set it right!"

### The Problem of Delay

Why doesn't Hamlet just do it? Four hundred years of criticism — the largest critical literature on any literary work in existence — has been, in one way or another, an answer to that question, and the play survives the arguments because Shakespeare planted evidence for every camp. The Romantic answer (Coleridge, Schlegel): Hamlet is overthinking, a "pure, noble, most moral nature" ruined by "an unbalanced kind of thinking"; the play is the portrait of thought as paralysis. The psychoanalytic answer (Freud, then Jones): Hamlet delays because Claudius has done what Hamlet unconsciously desired — killed the father, taken the mother — and the prince's revulsion is self-recognition. The political answer: revenge is treason; Hamlet must move carefully in a police state where Polonius plants spies in his own son's lodgings in Paris and children's theater troupes are vetted. The evidentiary answer is the play's own: Hamlet doubts the ghost, with excellent reason.

The play stages the doubt beautifully. Hamlet, ever the Wittenberg student, designs a test: "The play's the thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the King." But before the test, Shakespeare gives him the soliloquy that made the character immortal — "O that this too too sullied flesh would melt" — and then, later, the soliloquy that made the character a mirror: "To be, or not to be, that is the question." Both speeches are misreadings waiting to happen, and generations have obligingly misread them. "To be or not to be" is not a suicide note; read in context, it is a debate about whether conscious suffering ("to be") or action-under-ignorance ("to die... to sleep") is the sounder policy for a man who cannot know "what dreams may come." It is epistemology in pentameter — the play's whole argument, compressed.

### The Antic Disposition

Between the ghost's command and the play-within-the-play, Hamlet puts on a performance: he will be mad — "an antic disposition on" — and Shakespeare runs the strategy like an experiment with three variables. Against Polonius, his madness is a mirror: the old man is told he will be known by the shape of the cloud, by constructions as empty as his own intrigue, and he exits convinced the madness is love for Ophelia — because that is the data his ambition can use. Against Rosencrantz and Guildenstern, school friends turned instruments of the crown, the madness becomes the play's most famous speech about the human condition: "What a piece of work is a man! How noble in reason... and yet, to me, what is this quintessence of dust?" — delivered with the naked despair of a man who has stopped being able to feel the grandeur he can still describe. Against Ophelia, the performance is cruelty — "Get thee to a nunnery" — a scene whose ambiguity (is he performing? has his disgust with his mother overflowed onto all women? both?) has kept the scene at the center of feminist criticism for fifty years.

The trap in the strategy is that a mask, worn long enough in a court of spies, becomes indistinguishable from a face. Ophelia is the casualty who proves it. Forbidden by father and brother from seeing Hamlet, used by Polonius as bait in a staged meeting, then abandoned by her mad prince with his vilest performance, she loses father (killed by Hamlet, through the arras, mid-spying), lover, and standing in one act, and goes genuinely mad — singing snatches of songs about betrayal and sex, distributing flowers ("rosemary, that's for remembrance"), drowning in a brook among willows in the play's most lyrically reported death. Gertrude's account of the drowning — the willow, the "envious slivers," the songs half-sung as the water takes her — is the play's memorial to what Elsinore does to the innocent who stand between its men.

### The Mousetrap

The play-within-the-play is Hamlet's experiment, and its result is the closest thing the play has to a courtroom verdict. A troupe of traveling players arrives; Hamlet rewrites their murder play, The Murder of Gonzago, inserting a scenario that mirrors the ghost's story — poison poured in a garden, into an ear — and calls it The Mousetrap. "Have you heard the argument? Is there no offense in't?" he asks Claudius, with a straight face that is itself an essay on dramatic irony. The performance runs; the court watches; and at the moment the murderer pours the poison, Claudius rises, calls for lights, and exits. Hamlet, euphoric — "O good Horatio, I'll take the ghost's word for a thousand pound" — now has his evidence. Horatio, the play's one person of untempted integrity, confirms what he saw. The ghost told the truth. The delay is paid for. Action is now licensed.

And then Shakespeare pulls the cruelest structural joke in the canon: the very next scene, Hamlet finds Claudius alone, at prayer, unguarded — and cannot do it. Not because of doubt this time, but because of theology: killing a man mid-confession, with his soul "as fit for heaven," sends the murderer to salvation, and Hamlet wants damnation — soul and body. He sheathes the sword and goes to his mother's closet. (Unknown to him, Claudius can't pray: "My words fly up, my thoughts remain below.") It is the play's perfect miniature of its whole design: the one moment action would have been simple, Hamlet's reason over-architects it; the moment he leaves, the architecture collapses. Critics have called the prayer scene the most debated stage direction in literature; the play itself calls it fate administered by a thinking man.

### The Closet, the Letter, and the Voyage

Gertrude's chamber is where the play's private rot finally spills. Hamlet arrives to "speak daggers"; Polonius hides behind the arras to listen; the interview detonates — Gertrude frightened, crying murder, Polonius echoing, and Hamlet, feeling through the tapestry, runs his sword home and kills the wrong man. "Is it the King?" he asks, and the answer, "No, it is Polonius," is the hinge of the tragedy's second half: the revenger has now himself killed an innocent — or as innocent as Polonius ever was — and the play begins to collect the debts of his collateral damage. To his mother, in the scene's astonishing core, Hamlet delivers the comparison that defines the whole court: two portraits. Father Hyperion; Claudius a "mildewed ear." Then comes the sexual disgust that Freudians have mined ever since — "In the rank sweat of an enseamed bed, / Stewed in corruption, honeying and making love / Over the nasty sty."

The Ghost returns — visible only to Hamlet this time, "his father, in his habit as he lived" — to whet the "blunted purpose"; Gertrude sees nothing, and the scene leaves a permanent question about the whole play: is the ghost real, or has Hamlet's mind begun producing it? Shakespeare leaves the evidence as balanced as a scale. Then the cover-up begins: Hamlet hides the body ("The body is with the King, but the King is not with the body"), and Claudius, now knowing the prince is dangerous and popular ("He's loved of the distracted multitude"), ships him to England with a letter under seal — Rosencrantz and Guildenstern escorting — that instructs the English king to execute the bearer's head.

The voyage produces the play's great pivot toward competence. Hamlet, suspecting, gets the letter at night, and in a scene Shakespeare never stages but reports, forges the warrant: "an exact command... [that] they must not put upon the sledge" — substituting the escorts' names for his own, with an added request that the English give them "no shriving time allowed." He signs it as a king might. The line he uses to explain himself — "Why, man, they did make love to this employment; / They are not near my conscience" — marks how far the student has traveled: the man who agonized over killing one guilty king dispatches two former friends without a soliloquy. Pirates attack, and Hamlet boards the escaping ship in the melee. He is ransomed back to Denmark on a stolen letter, and he returns a different animal: "There's a divinity that shapes our ends, / Rough-hew them how we will."

### Ophelia, Laertes, and the Counter-Revenge

Meanwhile Elsinore has produced the play's structural counterweight: Laertes, Polonius's son, returns from France in a rage, storms the palace with a mob crying "Laertes shall be king," and finds his sister mad and his father buried "in secret." Claudius's management of Laertes is the play's study of how power recruits grief: within two scenes, the prince's double — a man with the same grievance Hamlet has — is converted into the Crown's instrument, a plot to kill Hamlet in a fencing match with an unbated, poisoned rapier, with a poisoned cup as backup. Laertes's fury is the mirror that shows what Hamlet's delay cost and what instant revenge looks like in Claudius's hands: immediate, dishonorable, and exactly what the play's world rewards.

Ophelia's death interrupts the plot with a disputed fact: did she drown by accident or by her own will? Gertrude's narrative says accident; the gravediggers' comic legalese says the question depends on whether she climbed to the willow or fell — and whether the water came to her or she to the water. The funeral that follows produces the play's most raw scene: Hamlet, returned, leaps into the grave to fight Laertes over who loved her more — "Forty thousand brothers could not, with all their quantity of love, / Make up my sum" — and the audience is left holding both truths at once: he is sincere, and his love destroyed her. The graveyard scene's other gift is the skull: Yorick, the jester who rode him piggyback, now "a pestilence of gore" — the play's meditation on mortality made flesh, literally, in the one object that turns Hamlet's metaphysics into a memory of a lap.

### The Final Bloodbath

The last scene is constructed like a machine for closing accounts. The fencing match, before the court, with the King's wager; the unbated point; the poison in the cup. Gertrude drinks to her son — Claudius's warning too late and too weak — and dies naming the drink. Laertes wounds Hamlet with the poisoned rapier; in the scuffle, the swords exchange hands, and Hamlet wounds Laertes with the same poison — the revenger and the avenger poisoned by the same instrument, Laertes crying "the king, the king's to blame" with his last breath. Hamlet finally kills Claudius — forcing the poisoned cup into his mouth — and the revenge the ghost demanded arrives at last, in the play's last ninety seconds, at the cost of everyone. "Had I but time — as this fell sergeant, Death, / Is strict in his arrest — O, I could tell you — / But let it be." The one man who might report the truth is Horatio, saved from drinking the dregs by Hamlet's dying plea: "Absent thee from felicity awhile... To tell my story." Fortinbras of Norway arrives to take the throne over a court of corpses, and the play ends with four captains bearing Hamlet "like a soldier" — the soldier's rites given to the one man in Denmark who behaved like a philosopher.

### Polonius: The Spy Who Runs the State's Nervous System

Every tragedy needs an atmosphere, and Hamlet's is manufactured by Polonius, the Lord Chamberlain — the play's most underrated engine. Shakespeare gives him the comedy and lets the comedy do the state's work: his maxims to Laertes ("Neither a borrower nor a lender be... This above all: to thine own self be true") are the period's pieties delivered in the period's clichés, and his espionage is industrial. He plants a spy on his own son in Paris; he uses his daughter as bait in a scripted encounter; he proposes to loose himself behind every conversation in the castle, including, fatally, the one in Gertrude's closet. The court of Elsinore is rotten not primarily because Claudius murdered his brother, but because it has been rebuilt as an intelligence operation in which father, son, friend, and lover are all simultaneously subjects and instruments.

Polonius's death scene is therefore the play's moral transfer point: the spy dies in the act of spying, yet the play marks the killing as Hamlet's first real crime — "I took thee for thy better." From this moment the revenger owes a debt to an innocent (or near-innocent) family, and the debt comes due in Ophelia's madness and Laertes' recruited rage. Shakespeare's engineering is exact: the state's corruption and the hero's contamination proceed on the same coordinate — the arras in the closet — and the audience, which wanted Hamlet to act for four acts, watches the action land on the wrong body and learns the price of wanting it.

### The Players: Theater Inside Theater

The arrival of the traveling players is a structural event disguised as a subplot. Hamlet welcomes them like old friends, quotes a speech of Aeneas's tale to Dido he has memorized ("The rugged Pyrrhus, like th' Hyrcanian beast"), and delivers the speech himself so beautifully that Polonius, philistine, begs the actor to stop. The lesson is quietly enormous: Hamlet can perform passion at will, at a moment's notice, to an audience of courtiers — so why can he not perform the part the ghost has written for him? His own answer, in the soliloquy that closes act two — "O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I... What's Hecuba to him, or he to Hecuba?" — turns the actor's art into an accusation: an actor can weep for a fictional queen; a son cannot act for a real father. The Mousetrap is born from this jealousy of theater's power, and Hamlet's advice to the players — "hold the mirror up to nature," never "o'erstep not the modesty of nature" — is Shakespeare leaving a manifesto for his own art inside the play that tests it.

The metatheater compounds in the Mousetrap itself: Hamlet and Horatio watch the court watch the play; Ophelia is seated in Hamlet's eyeline; the king's conscience is read from a seat in the dark. The scene teaches its audience how to watch — how performance confesses — and then dares you to apply the skill to Hamlet's own performances afterward, including his performance of madness. Shakespeare builds a court of amateurs reading each other's staging, and makes the audience members of it.

### Comedy in the Graveyard: The Gravediggers and Yorick

Act five opens with a scene that should not work and is the play's most tender: two gravediggers, trading legal jokes about whether Ophelia's drowned body qualifies for Christian burial — "Give me leave. Here lies the water — good. Here stands the man — good. If the water come to him, he drowns not himself" — while tossing skulls with a spade. Shakespeare puts the play's heaviest question (what is a death worth, legally, socially, finally?) in the mouths of laborers, and lets their jokes land harder than the court's theology. The scene's pivot, from humor into memory, is the Yorick skull: Hamlet takes the jester's skull from the earth, and twenty-three years of a childhood — rides on his back, "infinite jest," kisses — collapse into "a pestilence of gore." It is the moment the play's metaphysics acquire a face, and the soliloquy that follows ("Alas, poor Yorick") turns Alexander and Caesar into plug and loam with a gravedigger's economy.

The scene also stages the play's one pure encounter between classes: Hamlet, prince, bargaining with a gravedigger, who outwits him verbally for a page and a half and is the only person in Denmark who speaks to him without fear or strategy. That Hamlet meets honest language only among the dead and the diggers of the dead is the play's quietest verdict on Elsinore.

### Why It Remains the Summit

Hamlet's afterlife is the largest of any secular work: some fifty adaptations and derived plays before 1900, the longest role in Shakespeare (roughly 1,500 lines), and a grip on the language — "to be or not to be," "the rest is silence," "something is rotten in the state of Denmark," "though this be madness, yet there is method in't," "brevity is the soul of wit" — that makes it, phrase by phrase, the most quoted work in English. The reason is structural, not merely verbal: Shakespeare took the revenge plot — the engine of half the drama of his age — and installed inside it a mind that examines the plot's premises. The result is the founding document of dramatic interiority. Before Hamlet, protagonists did; after Hamlet, protagonists wonder whether to do, and the theater of the last four centuries — Ibsen, Chekhov, Beckett — descends from the wondering.

The play's instruction, read now, is about the cost of standards in a corrupted environment. Hamlet's tragedy is not that he thinks too much or too little; it is that he is asked to act on incomplete information inside a system designed to make certainty impossible, where every tool he touches — madness, theater, friendship, the ghost itself — is also a tool the system uses. "The time is out of joint" is his diagnosis, and the play's genius is to ask, without answering, whether one man born into a broken time owes it repair, rebellion, or performance — and whether any of the three can be done with clean hands.

### Rosencrantz and Guildenstern: The Comedy of the Replaceable

Shakespeare's treatment of the two school friends is a small masterpiece of moral accounting that productions too often rush. Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are not villains; they are interchangeable — the text has fun with their indistinguishability (Claudius assigns them as a pair; even Gertrude swaps their names) — and that interchangeability is the point. They are what a surveillance court produces in bulk: amiable men whose ambition is comfort, who accept the errand ("so much as from occasion you may glean") without asking what happens to the errand's object. Hamlet's recorder speech to Guildenstern — "You would play upon me... you cannot play upon me" — is the play's most underrated scene: it defines the crime as using a person as an instrument, and it is the exact crime Hamlet himself then commits in reverse by forging their death warrant. Shakespeare does not let the hero escape the symmetry; Hamlet half-admits it ("they are not near my conscience"), and the audience is left to weigh whether conscience that scales with grievance is conscience at all. Tom Stoppard built an entire classic play from these two walk-ons precisely because the canon's most examined mind has its most modern moral problem parked in these minor characters.

#KEY_IDEAS
- The play's revolution is structural: a revenge plot given to a hero with legitimate doubts about his evidence — action drama becomes epistemology.
- "To be or not to be" is not a suicide note; it is a cost-benefit analysis of conscious suffering versus ignorant action, and it still stands as philosophy in verse.
- The Mousetrap is theater as an instrument of justice — and its success only deepens the tragedy, since evidence does not repair a rotten state.
- Masks become faces: Hamlet's antic disposition manufactures real casualties, and Ophelia is the price of his experiment.
- The delay's true cost is counted in collateral: Polonius, Ophelia, Rosencrantz, Guildenstern — the innocent and the near-innocent who die in a revenger's blast radius.
- Hamlet and Laertes are designed doubles: instant revenge recruited by power versus delayed revenge eaten by thought — the play refuses to declare a winner.

#HOW_TO_READ
Read the first scene slowly — sentries, dread, rumor of war preparations — because Shakespeare builds the play's atmosphere before its plot. Track the soliloquies against what Hamlet does immediately afterward; the play constantly stages the gap between his rhetoric and his action. Watch Polonius with respect: his espionage is the play's picture of how a surveillance state actually works, and his death is its first proof that spy systems kill their own. Read Ophelia's scenes as a continuous story, not interludes. In performance, the ghost decides the production — seen by all (past) or seen by Hamlet alone (psychological) — so decide which play you are reading. End with the gravediggers: comedy is doing the play's hardest thinking about death.

#REFLECTION
1. Hamlet demands certainty before acting, and the delay has a body count. Where in your life is the standard of "more evidence" quietly serving avoidance?
2. The Mousetrap works: staged truth catches a real confession. What are the ethical limits of setting traps for people you distrust?
3. Laertes and Hamlet share a grievance; one is recruited by power and one is destroyed by reflection. Which failure mode is closer to yours?
4. "There's a divinity that shapes our ends" — Hamlet's late surrender of control. What would it cost you to hold your plans that loosely?
