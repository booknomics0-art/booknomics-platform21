#SUMMARY
### Nick Carraway: The Narrator Who Promises to Judge No One

The Great Gatsby is told by Nick Carraway, and the first page is a contract he will spend the entire book violating. His father's advice — "Whenever you feel like criticizing any one... just remember that all the people in this world haven't had the advantages that you've had" — produces Nick's claim to be "inclined to reserve all judgments," and within three chapters he has judged everyone, including himself. This is not a flaw in the novel; it is the novel. Fitzgerald built his masterpiece on an unreliable, self-flattering, morally squeamish narrator through whose softening lens the whole story reaches us, and the book's deepest question — the one that has kept critics arguing for a century — is what actually happened when Nick wasn't editing.

Nick is a bond salesman from the Midwest, thirty, a Yale man with "one of those confident promises" of a limitless future, who rents a cheap house in West Egg, Long Island, in the summer of 1922. Next door stands a mansion that looks like a fairground copy of a French town hall — "a factual imitation of some Hôtel de Ville in Normandy" — and the copy is the theme. Everything in the novel's world is a reproduction: the books in Gatsby's library are real books with uncut pages, purchased by the yard; the "Oxford man" is an Oxford man by five months of a wartime training program; even the host's name is a costume worn by a North Dakota farm boy named James Gatz. The novel's world is the first fully rendered consumer economy in American literature — a place where identity itself can be purchased — and Fitzgerald's genius is to admire the purchase and mourn it in the same sentence.

The setting is a machine. West Egg, the new money side of the bay, faces East Egg across a "courtesy bay" of water — old money, inherited, the home of Tom and Daisy Buchanan, Nick's cousin and her husband. Between the Eggs and Manhattan runs the Valley of Ashes, the dumping ground of the industrial economy where George Wilson repairs cars that carry the rich past his window. The geography is a diagram: dream (West Egg), aristocracy (East Egg), the human cost (the Valley), and the city where money multiplies at night. Almost every event in the novel travels this road, and the deaths arrive on it.

### Tom and Daisy: The Careless People

Tom Buchanan is the novel's exhibit of inherited power: polo ponies, a string of polio-glazed old-money certainty, a body "cruel," a mind of "smouldering" resentment dressed as thought — his famous rants about the rising colored races come from a book called The Rise of the Colored Empires, real pseudo-science of the 1920s that Fitzgerald leaves in for a reason. Tom keeps a mistress in the city — Myrtle Wilson, the garage keeper's wife — in an apartment he pays for like a utility, and when she says Daisy's name at his party, he breaks her nose. Fitzgerald gives Tom no irony and no privacy: his violence is casual, his cruelty is ambient, and his self-pity, when it finally arrives in the Plaza hotel room, is immense.

Daisy is the novel's most contested character, deliberately drawn at the edge of visibility — we mostly see her through Gatsby's idealization and Nick's condescension, which is to say through two layers of projection. Her voice is her signature: "the kind of voice that the ear follows up and down, as if each speech is an arrangement of notes that will never be played again" — and Gatsby's correction, the novel's most quoted line of characterization: "Her voice is full of money." Fitzgerald hangs the entire tragedy on that identification. Daisy is not evil; she is a woman of ordinary nerve raised inside a system that taught her the world exists to be comfortable in, and her only demonstrated loyalty, in the whole book, is to that comfort. Her daughter Pammy appears for one scene, held up for admiration like a doll — "The most wonderful little girl" — and the sight of the child sends Gatsby into a trance, because the child is living proof that time has moved on without him.

The Buchanans' role in the plot is small and their role in the meaning is total. When the novel's violence finally touches them, their response is described by Nick in the sentence the whole book has been preparing: "They were careless people, Tom and Daisy — they smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money or their vast carelessness, or whatever it was that kept them together, and let other people clean up the mess they had made." Fitzgerald's class critique is complete in that retreat: the book's criminals are not its bootleggers, they are its inheritors.

### The Parties and the Man Behind Them

The first half of the novel is famous for its parties, and it is easy to miss how strange they are: three parties in three weeks — Tom's apartment, Myrtle's; then Buchanans' dinner; then Gatsby's gardens — each one bigger, each one emptier. Gatsby's parties are described with a sensory generosity that conceals a structural absence: hundreds of guests who "conducted themselves according to the rules of behavior associated with amusement parks," who arrive without invitation, know nothing of their host, and exchange his biography as rumor — German spy, killer, nephew of Kaiser Wilhelm, the man who "killed a man once." The parties are performances without a script's author visible, and the guests consume a man they have invented in his absence.

Nick's introduction to Gatsby — a courteous stranger at his own party, then the revelation weeks later — begins the book's second movement: Gatsby's biography, told in fragments out of order, like testimony assembled by a careful lawyer. Jay Gatsby is James Gatz, seventeen, of "shiftless and unsuccessful farm people," who invented "Jay Gatsby" with the same precision another boy might build a model airplane — "a universe of ineffable gaudiness" that required constant service. His mentor is Dan Cody, a copper tycoon whose yacht he sails as "steward, mate, skipper, secretary," and whose fortune never reaches him — Ella Kaye, the newspaper woman, gets it — but whose real inheritance is permanent: the education of a gentleman's surface. What Gatsby lacks, and what his whole adult life is engineered to manufacture, is standing: the thing that cannot be earned, only born into — except that Gatsby, the novel's founding act of faith, does not accept the exception.

### The Green Light and the Architecture of Longing

The novel's most famous image arrives at the end of chapter 1, almost casually: Gatsby on his dock at night, "trembling," arms toward a green light at the end of Daisy's dock across the bay. Nick does not yet know what it means; by the time we learn the light is Daisy's, the image has already done its work. The green light is the novel in miniature — visible, exact, near, and unreachable; a signal that appears to be aimed at you and belongs to a world on the far side of water. When Gatsby finally brings Daisy to his mansion and shows her his shirts, weeping into the "beautiful shirts" as she piles them, the scene's absurdity is the point: the wealth has no purpose except as proof of a self that can walk into Daisy's world — and Daisy, confronted with the proof, cries, because that is the only response her life has equipped her with.

Fitzgerald's prose in these chapters is the book's other celebrity, and it deserves the reputation: the man who could write "the whole caravansary had fallen in like a card house at the disapproval in her eyes" was writing the American language at the edge of what it could carry. But the purple passages are load-bearing, not decorative. Gatsby's world is sustained by style — the parties, the shirts, the pink suit, the "old sport" — and the style is a translation of yearning into purchase. When the prose soars, Fitzgerald is showing you what the money is buying; when it crashes into Tom's tennis logic or Daisy's yawns, he is showing you what money cannot.

### The Reunion and the Clock

The reunion scene — arranged by Nick, tea at his cottage, Gatsby so nervous he knocks a "defunct mantelpiece clock" from the mantel and catches it — compresses the book into one image: a man catching time as it falls, setting it back where it was, and pretending it never broke. Gatsby wants the five years retracted. "Can't repeat the past?" he cries to Nick. "Why of course you can!" The novel's answer unfolds over its final third with the patience of a court verdict: the past can be repeated only if everything it changed is also retracted — the child, the marriage, the money's origin, Daisy's own aging — and Daisy, who genuinely loved Gatsby once, cannot pay. Her crisis in the Plaza is not between two men but between two speeds of time: Gatsby's frozen five years and Tom's disposable decades. She chooses the speed she can survive at.

### The Plaza Confrontation

The book's showdown is staged with the precision of a chess problem: a birthday (Nick's thirtieth, "the promise of a decade of loneliness"), the hottest day of the year, a drive to Manhattan in two cars — Gatsby and Daisy together, in Tom's blue coupe of a joke, past Wilson's garage where Myrtle watches from the window and misidentifies the yellow car's owner. At the Plaza hotel, Tom begins the teardown that is his class's specialty: not fistfights but footnotes. Where did Gatsby's money come from? Drugstores — Walter Chase, the bond scheme; "swindler" — and each fact is aimed less at Daisy than at Daisy's imagination of a life. Gatsby's counterattack — tell Tom you never loved him — asks Daisy to delete her own history, and she cannot do it, even for love. "I did love him once — but I loved you too." The sentence breaks Gatsby more than Tom's accusations do, because Gatsby's entire architecture requires that the five years be blank.

Tom, sensing the win, orders everyone home — changing the drivers, putting Daisy in Gatsby's yellow car with Gatsby at the wheel. It is the novel's single most consequential act of carelessness: Tom is not sending his wife off with a killer; he is simply ending an uncomfortable scene. What the yellow car meets, on its way back through the Valley of Ashes, is Myrtle Wilson, locked upstairs by her husband, who has discovered the affair and is preparing to move west. She bolts for a car she believes Tom is driving — the same car that passed earlier that afternoon — and dies on the road, her mouth "torn," one breast swinging loose, "the death car" vanishing toward the Eggs.

### The Crash and the Funeral

The final movement is a study in how wealth metabolizes catastrophe. Gatsby's first act after the killing is protective — he watches all night outside the Buchanans' house, guarding Daisy from Tom's "violence." Daisy's first act is to turn off the lights and have supper with her husband, "cold dishes of cod" on the table, while across the bay her protector sits in the dark of a swimming pool no one has used all summer. Wilson, following the car through the city, finds Tom first, then the yellow car's address; and the next morning he shoots Gatsby in his pool and himself in the grass. Fitzgerald renders the death in one unbearable domestic image: the gardener's request to drain the pool, Gatsby's "don't do it today... I've never used it all summer," and the man floating, dead, in his one unperformed luxury.

The funeral is the novel's verdict delivered as a guest list: nobody comes. The hundreds who consumed his hospitality send messages, excuses, nothing; Wolfsheim, the gangster who "made him," cannot attend because he is "a man of science" and has "turned the corner"; Daisy sends no message at all — she and Tom have gone off with a bag of clothes, traveling, vanishing. Only Nick, Gatsby's father (who arrives with the schedule the boy Gatz had pinned inside his copy of Hopalong Cassidy — "one hour of gymnastics daily... study needed inventions"), the owl-eyed man from the library, and a handful of servants stand in the rain. The schedule — a farm boy's self-improvement plan with no crime in it at all — is Fitzgerald's final, devastating piece of evidence in Gatsby's defense: the dream's origin was clean. What corrupted it was the world it was trying to enter.

### Jordan Baker, Golf, and the Ethics of the Leisure Class

Between the parties and the plot, Fitzgerald distributes a quiet indictment through Nick's romance with Jordan Baker, the professional golfer with an "absurd, charming little laugh" and a body "of an athlete" held "sleepily" in balance. Jordan is introduced in the novel's first chapter curled in the Buchanans' drawing room, and by the third chapter Nick remembers where he has heard her name: her golf tournament was whispered into scandal the year before — a ball moved in a match — and the whisper "reached" Nick as he stood beside her at Gatsby's party. The novel's leisure class is audited in small files like this: Tom's reading, Daisy's voice, Jordan's ball. None of it is criminal; all of it is the same muscle — the assumption that consequences are for other people — flexed in its different gyms.

Nick's famous self-description — "I am one of the few honest people that I have ever known" — arrives directly after his account of holding Jordan's hand through his lies about where his apartment is, and the book asks you to notice the collision. Everyone in Gatsby lies; the distinction the novel actually draws is between lying to build (Gatsby, whose fabrication serves a dream) and lying to float (the Buchanans' circle, whose fabrications serve comfort). Jordan belongs to the second kind, and Nick's capitulation to her — he decides he is in love, or decides it is practical, the text will not say — is his own small rehearsal of the temptation the whole novel studies: to stop weighing things because the scales are tiresome.

### Meyer Wolfsheim and the Machinery Under the Party

Gatsby's underworld is sketched with a few strokes of enormous precision, and the most famous is Meyer Wolfsheim, "the man who fixed the World's Series back in 1919" — Fitzgerald, writing in 1924, put the Black Sox scandal into fiction within three years of the event, as a business card. Wolfsheim's cufflinks are human molars; his luncheon conversation is about "business gonnegtion" and a murdered friend shot in the stomach "three times" by strangers; and Gatsby's defense of him to Nick — "He's the man who fixed the World's Series" said with evident pride — is the novel's sharpest clue to how its hero's fortune was assembled. The parties are the surface of a bootlegging-and-bond-scheme apparatus; drugstores selling grain alcohol over the counter; Walter Chase, the commissioner, bought; a young man found dead in the woods after taking a ride in Gatsby's car.

The detail matters because the novel refuses the romance of the self-made man while honoring the dreamer. Gatsby's criminality is real and almost incidental: he is a modest crook and an enormous idealist, and the novel's snobbiest characters — Tom at the Plaza — use precisely the criminality Tom's own class profits from (his "god-awful" bootlegger friends appear in his own dinner conversations) as a status weapon. Fitzgerald's ledger is exact: the same economy that lets Tom inherit lets Gatsby purchase; the difference between them is only which side of the bay the purchase sits on, and which one the law can find.

### The Eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg

Above the Valley of Ashes, on a forgotten billboard, hang the enormous eyes of an oculist whose practice has "sank down here into an eternal blindness" — a pair of yellow spectacles over a vanishing face, the novel's most debated symbol. Fitzgerald uses the eyes as the book's absent god: they stare down the road where Tom breaks Myrtle's nose, where Nick meets Tom's mistress, and where Myrtle dies under the wheels of the yellow car. Wilson, broken by his wife's death, stares at the billboard and tells the eyes: "God sees everything" — and Tom, in the novel's last meeting with Nick, will dismiss the comparison with the comfort of a man who has never been watched by anything: "And if it runs out I can stop at a drug-store and buy some."

The symbol's power is in its hollowness. The novel offers no providence; the eyes are paint, the face behind them has worn away, and the only judgment the book records is administered by the living — Nick's, mostly, and the reader's. That a novel saturated in parties and shirts closes with a corpse on a mortuary table under a billboard is Fitzgerald's final arrangement of his world: transcendence has been replaced by advertising, and the advertisement outlasts the belief.

### Nick's Verdict and the Novel's Afterlife

The last pages perform Nick's judgment on himself as much as on the Buchanans: his affair with Jordan ends the way it lived, in carelessness; his bond business and his Midwest return are renunciations of the entire East; and his famous closing — boats against the current, "borne back ceaselessly into the past" — universalizes Gatsby's doom into a national one. The green light, the fresh green breast of the new world that Dutch sailors' eyes first met, the orgastic future that recedes: Fitzgerald closes the novel by turning a love story into a reading of America itself, the country founded on the belief that the past can be outrun.

The novel's history is its own Gatsby arc: a commercial failure in 1925 (two small printings, Fitzgerald's earnings a few thousand dollars), rescued posthumously — republished in 1941 after his death and canonized by a generation of critics, then by the war's Armed Services Editions that distributed a hundred and twenty-three thousand copies to soldiers. By mid-century it was the American novel. Its afterlife has matched its subject: five film adaptations, an opera, a ballet, ten thousand school syllabi, and a permanent place in the national vocabulary — "old sport," the green light, the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg watching the ash heaps from a discarded billboard. Every generation rediscovers the same precision in it: the way it locates the exact distance between wanting a life and being able to buy one, and names, in the Buchanans, the class that the distance protects.

The novel's instruction, for a reader now, is double. It is a warning about the dreamer: Gatsby's tragedy is that he mistook a woman for an ideal and a fortune for a self — the old "colossal vitality of his illusion" that Daisy "tumbled short of," the "incarnation" complete. And it is a warning about the beneficiaries: the world that let him build, that consumed his hospitality, and that retreated from his funeral into its money without pausing. "They're a rotten crowd," Nick tells Gatsby, in the book's most tender and most hopeless scene. "You're worth the whole damn bunch put together" — the one honest compliment in the novel, delivered to a man who cannot hear it as anything but failure.

### The Title's Irony: Greatness, Earned and Ironic

The title deserves its own audit, because "Great" is the book's most loaded word. Nick's narration applies it with visible hesitation — Gatsby is "The Great" the way a fairground act is great: the label on the billboard of his self-invention, adopted before Nick has any evidence. And then, incrementally, the evidence arrives and the irony doubles back: Gatsby is great, exactly as advertised, in the Victorian sense the 1920s had mislaid — great in constancy, great in the scale of his hope, "great" in the sense of a man who organized five years of a life around a single ideal and never once broke the organization. Fitzgerald's title is a trap for cynics. The reader who finishes the book certain that Gatsby was a fool has missed Nick's last verdict ("They're a rotten crowd... You're worth the whole damn bunch put together") and missed the novel's structural sympathy: the crook with the schedule and the dream is the only character in the book who wants anything. Everyone else wants comfort. In Fitzgerald's ledger, a corrupt idealist outranks a clean hedonist, and "greatness" is measured by the size of what a person is willing to be ruined for.

#KEY_IDEAS
- The novel's real subject is the difference between a self that is built and a self that is inherited — and the world that punishes only one of those methods.
- "Her voice is full of money": Fitzgerald makes desire and class inseparable; Gatsby loves a person as a doorway to a caste.
- Carelessness is the book's great crime: "they smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money."
- The green light is longing itself — exact, visible, and structurally unreachable; bringing Daisy to his mansion is Gatsby's attempt to move the light.
- The past cannot be repeated because it contains people who have changed — Daisy's refusal to "delete" her history is the novel's most humane moment.
- Gatsby's boyhood schedule is the book's hidden heart: the dream began clean; the corruption was the toll charged by the world it sought to enter.

#HOW_TO_READ
Read Nick as a character, not a window: track what he claims ("I'm one of the few honest people") against what he does, and you will find the novel's second story, the one he is not aware of telling. Read the parties as economic events — notice who works them, who performs, who pays. Read the Plaza scene twice: the first time for the plot, the second for Tom's method — how inherited power wins arguments with footnotes and logistics rather than ideas. Then read chapter 9's funeral as the class audit it is. Save the last page for last and slow: the prose is doing philosophy at speed, and it is the most quoted paragraph in American literature for a reason.

#REFLECTION
1. Gatsby organized his entire life around recovering one moment. Which of your own "green lights" is a signal you have misread — aimed, perhaps, at someone else's shore?
2. Where does your world — work, family, market — reward the "carelessness" of the Buchanans: damage created and exited without cleanup?
3. Gatsby's self-invention was disciplined and, at root, clean. What is the difference between a costume and a character — and which are you wearing today?
4. Daisy chooses survival over romance. In her position and her era, was any other choice actually available — and what does your answer say about how you price loyalty?
